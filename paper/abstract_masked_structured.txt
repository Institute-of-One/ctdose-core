Background: Retrospective CT dose work on public imaging archives faces a rarely quantified obstacle: the recorded dose index (CTDIvol) is frequently absent from the image header, and no radiation dose structured report accompanies de-identified data, leaving no dose value to work from.

Purpose: We present an open-source, provenance-aware framework that recovers dose indices from the acquisition metadata that survives de-identification, distinguishes recovered values from recorded ones at every step, and makes the limits of that recovery measurable.

Methods: An open-source Python package (MIT License) computes CTDIvol, dose-length product (DLP), size-specific dose estimate (SSDE) and effective dose. Every value carries provenance at four levels — formula, inputs, assumptions, and a source classifying it as recorded, reconstructed, derived or unrecoverable — so the four are never interchangeable by accident. A vendor-neutral parser reads the CT Radiation Dose Structured Report (RDSR) from DICOM coded content items; where no recorded index exists, CTDIvol is rebuilt from tube current, rotation time, collimation and pitch against an openly licensed table of measured normalised CTDI values, with DLP and effective dose following from the imaged scan length and published conversion factors. SSDE follows AAPM TG-204 from an automatically contoured water-equivalent diameter. We evaluated the framework on 14 real RDSR objects from four manufacturers and on 400 public CT series from The Cancer Imaging Archive, 100 per manufacturer across 92 collections. No imaging is redistributed.

Results: The parser identified and parsed all 14 RDSR objects and rejected one secondary-capture dose screen, spanning 4 manufacturers, 9 scanner models and 67 irradiation events; two independent consistency checks passed on 14 of 14 files. In the archive sample, CTDIvol was absent from the image header in 206 of 400 series (51.5%), at rates strongly associated with manufacturer, from 24.0% to 100.0%. Of those 206, 42 (20.4%) retained enough metadata and a scanner in the coefficient table to reconstruct one (GE 37.0%, Canon/Toshiba 20.8%, Philips and Siemens 0%); by either route 236 of 400 (59.0%) were dose-recoverable, leaving 164 (41.0%) unrecoverable. A further 122 were described completely enough to reconstruct but sat on a scanner no open measurement covers. Against the scanner's own recorded values (n = 46; Canon/Toshiba 27, Siemens 12, Philips 7, GE 0, no GE series carrying a recorded value to compare against), the reconstruction had a median relative error of −8.7%, and −5.7% (95% CI −12.4% to +7.8%) in the stratum recording the total collimation, with 60.0% within ±20%.

Conclusion: Dose indices can be recovered from acquisition metadata when no recorded value survives, but only as far as that metadata and the available open measurements allow, and a recovered value is not interchangeable with a recorded one. Provenance makes that distinction explicit and the residual uncertainty attributable. Availability was strongly associated with manufacturer and roughly four in ten series admitted no estimate at all. Dose-index availability should be reported by manufacturer, while reconstruction accuracy should be stratified by manufacturer and metadata completeness whenever the available sample supports such analysis.
